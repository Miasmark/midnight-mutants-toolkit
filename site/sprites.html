Atari 7800 · Midnight Mutants · 1990

Sprite Sheet

Every actor cell rendered straight out of the cartridge. Both pages are now fully identified — thirty-two from the disassembly, the rest by eye against the running game. One block of six bytes is referenced by nothing in the ROM at all, which took proving rather than looking.

How these were extracted

MARIA stores graphics line-planar, so a sprite is a column through consecutive pages.

A display-list entry points at (high << 8) | low, and scanline n of the zone reads page high+n at the same low byte. So a sprite 4 bytes wide and 16 lines tall occupies pages high through high+15 at bytes low to low+3 — but the zone offset counts down, so the first scanline reads the highest page and the last reads the base. Rendering the pages in ascending order turns every sprite upside down. The lettering is the giveaway: $D8/$DC spell GA·ME and $F8/$FC spell OV·ER only when flipped.

Actors write $E0 into the entry's high byte, and a few write $C0 — both in bank 7. In 160×2 mode each byte is four 2-bit pixels, so these cells are 16×16. Colour comes from the MARIA palette registers at run time (actors use palette $BE, specials $BC), so this renders the raw 2-bit indices as a grey ramp rather than inventing colours. Index 0 is transparent.

Page $C0 is the player, and the zombies borrow his legs. The player is drawn as a top half plus a set of legs — vertical movement $00-$1C over legs $20-$30, horizontal $40-$4C over legs $60-$7C, with separate attack tops at $34 and $50. Those same leg cells serve the zombies, which is how one page carries both: from $90 up it is zombie heads and torsos, and the variants are region-specific — $E0/$E4 in Dr Evil's ground, $E8/$EC the pair outside the lab, and $F0-$FC the humpbacked zombie whose flat 16-point contact hit makes the caves punishing.
$28 belongs to the undead death. The player's sprite selector $5E indexes dat_455C, the same composite-record table the actors use. For $10/$20 — a blood death without the cross — the record names page $E0 cells $10/$14 plus $28 — a human head and shoulders in palette 2, sitting +3,-8 from the body. It is drawn over the bat you turn into rather than standing alone, and nothing else in the game uses it, which is why it sat unidentified through the whole survey. Which is the game's premise in one byte: drained of blood, you become the thing that drained you, unless you were carrying the cross.
One block is referenced by nothing at all. Six bytes, $88-$8D — the $88 cell and the left half of $8C — hold 81 lit pixels that read as a pair of long thin legs, sitting exactly one wolf-bottom slot before the wolf's own run at $8E. Every sprite source in the ROM was enumerated by finding each store to a graphics byte and tracing the load feeding it, and none of them names it; in play, with every spawn slot filled and the wolf driven through all fifteen frames, nothing draws it either. A discarded wolf frame whose top no longer exists is the best reading, but it composes with none of the three surviving tops — so that is a reading, not a finding. Being unreferenced is what makes it reusable: the editor's fourth axe pose lives at $88.
$E0-$EE is no longer a guess. It was captioned as a melting death animation; the composite records settle it. Records $40-$4E draw the wolf and name $E0-$EA for its death, and the special actors' death records $64/$65 borrow $E4-$EE from the same run — which is how the item-select reticle at $EE ends up drawn as a corpse's right leg.
The frame tables are tiny. dat_5CE2 is four bytes and dat_5E6A twenty, so the ordinary-actor draw path selects from a handful of cells by facing, not from a per-creature table. The artwork says why that is enough: creatures are assembled from parts, and the parts are shared. The zombies reuse the player's legs and differ only in the head, so a region's cast can be changed by pointing at different head cells rather than by carrying a whole sprite set per enemy.

Appearance, kind and room are one chain

Where the artwork and the disassembly finally meet.

sub_63F5 builds a head sprite from two values: 

LDA sactor_facing,X
ASL A : ASL A          ; facing * 4
ADC sactor_kind,X      ; + kind
TAX
LDA dat_6440,X         ; the head sprite's low byte
STA disp_gfx,Y

That table holds odd offsets — $A2, $AA, $B6, $BA, each two past a cell boundary. The game addresses the right-hand half of a cell directly, which is the code corroborating the split packing found by eye.

The kind itself comes from the room. PickSactorKind reads tbl_ScreenKind, one byte per room: bit 7 clear and the value is the kind; bit 7 set and the low three bits are a mask for a random draw. So a room's single byte fixes its cast — and through the same kind index it also fixes toughness and palette.

Ordinary actors keep no hit points: actor_mode is the state, seeded per kind and pushed toward $A0 by weapon_level + 1 per hit. Special actors are the four-slot cast with a flat $10 contact hit and their own damage byte.

The cells

Both pages, every non-empty 4-byte cell.

named by the disassembly identified by eye against the running game not identified

Some cells hold two sprites side by side. A cell is 4 bytes = 16 pixels, and much of page $C0 packs an 8-pixel sprite in bytes 0–1 and another in bytes 2–3. Twenty-seven cells are split that way, shown here divided by a dashed rule. The item icons on page $E0 are packed the same way.

The head block is interleaved, not sequential. $A0–$BC is two cells per facing, and within each group the four halves run in a fixed order: bignose, a spare slot, longneck, hunchback. Three types carry all four facings. The spare slot is recycled for the types that do not need four — the headless zombie and the bulb-head have one head apiece for every direction, and the pumpkin-head has two — so nothing in the block is wasted.

The pumpkin-head's two heads sit in the towards and away groups but face the opposite way to the group they are packed with, which is the sort of detail that only shows up from play.

A creature taller than 16 pixels is built from two cells stacked — the ghost is $38/$3C over $58/$5C, and its death animation is $20/$24 over $40/$44, with the waving arm gone from the lower half. The wolf runs the same way, five top frames from $6C paired with five bottom frames from $8C, and in both rows one cell ($78, $98) carries two half-frames side by side.

The item icons are packed two to a cell, since each is 8 pixels wide against the cell's 16: $D0 is the heart and necklace, $D4 the cross and crypt key, $F0 the diamond and a potion, $F4 the plasmic pumpkin and the lantern.

Page $E0 is not only actors. It carries the win screen too: Grampa at $30/$34 and $50/$54, his legs at $C8/$CC, and his speech bubble reading AWE·SOME at $C0/$C4. $D8 $DC $F8 $FC are the GAME OVER banner. $C8 holds the last column of the bubble and bleeds into its neighbour, which is normal for artwork packed to the byte.
Six bytes in the wolf's row that nothing draws. $88–$8D holds artwork — 81 lit pixels, reading as a pair of long thin legs — and no reference reaches it. The wolf's bottoms are six bytes wide and run $8E, $94, $9A, $9F, paired with tops $6E, $74, $7A by composite records $40–$42. So this block is exactly one wolf-bottom slot immediately before that run, and the left half of the $8C cell falls inside it while $8E onward is the wolf proper.

Checked two ways. Statically: no sprite table, composite record, icon table or immediate names it. In play: with every spawn slot filled, throwing on and the wolf driven through all fifteen of its animation frames, the pairs drawn in that stretch are $6E $74 $7A and $8E $94 $9A $9F and nothing else.

It is most consistent with a discarded wolf frame whose top no longer exists — but it does not compose convincingly with any of the three surviving tops, so that is a reading, not a finding.

Being unreferenced makes it usable: an axe projectile sprite is two bytes wide, so $88 is a free slot. The editor's fourth axe pose mirrors $CE into it, giving that animation four distinct orientations instead of three.

The weapon shots, resolved. A projectile draws dat_4FAE[dat_4F9E[type] + phase] with the phase counting down from dat_4FD2[type], which is a frame count. Reading the three tables gives every weapon's animation exactly:

So $CE is not a decorative inverted axe but the axe projectile facing up, and the axe is the only weapon that never animates — one sprite for the whole flight, which is why it appears to slide.

The mega blaster's animation was drawn and never wired. It asks for four phases, but its run reads $A4 $A6 $A4 $A6 — two pictures shown twice. $A8 and $AA hold two more frames of the same glowing orb, 65 and 74 lit pixels, with the bright core in a different place, and nothing on page $E0 names them. dat_6440 does contain those two values, which is what makes them easy to miss, but that table feeds the special-actor slot and f6:$65B1 gives that slot page $C0 — a different page and different artwork. Pointing the last two phases at the drawn cells costs two bytes.

$A0–$A6 were previously captioned as wolf death frames. They are not: the wolf is drawn from composite records $40–$4E, which name $6E $74 $7A and $8E $94 $9A $9F for its body and $E0–$EA for its death. $A8/$AA are named by nothing at all.
